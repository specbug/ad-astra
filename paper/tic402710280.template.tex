\begin{abstract}
Transiting sub-Neptunes on orbits longer than a month are rare around bright stars. Here we report a candidate with a 36.34-day period around \tic, a $V = 10.0$ K dwarf at 66.8~pc that the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (\tess) has observed only in full-frame images. Four transits from 2021 to 2026, reduced by three independent pipelines, have consistent depths (mean $@@DEPTH@@$~ppm) and a duration of $@@T14@@$~h. Two fall in consecutive orbits, and the transits expected at every shorter period are absent at $\geq @@ALIAS_MIN@@\sigma$, which fixes the period at $P = @@P@@$~d. The candidate has a radius of $@@RP@@\,\rearth$ and an equilibrium temperature of $@@TEQ@@$~K. Difference-image centroids place the signal $1.5'' \pm 1.3''$ from the target, and eclipse models of the only nearby \gaia\ source, $3.5''$ away, fit worse than a planet by $\Delta\chi^2 \geq 142$ for every main-sequence pair. No secondary eclipse is detected, and \texttt{TRICERATOPS} gives a false-positive probability of $0.003 \pm 0.004$; without high-resolution imaging, the object remains a candidate. The transit times scatter about a linear ephemeris by @@RMS@@~min (rms). Light curves from an independent pipeline reproduce the largest departure, but four transits cannot separate timing systematics from transit-timing variations. A second signal, of $@@SS_DEPTH@@$~ppm with a period of 3.78~days, may come from the target, an unresolved companion, or the background star. We predict a transit in \tess\ Sector~114 on 2027 February~19.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Transiting sub-Neptunes on orbits longer than a month are rare around bright stars. The NASA Exoplanet Archive lists six confirmed transiting planets of 3--4\,$\rearth$ around K dwarfs ($T_{\rm eff} = 3900$--5300~K) brighter than $V = 10.5$, and only one of them, TOI-2076~d \citep[$P = 35.1$~d;][]{Hedges2021}, has a period longer than 30~days \citep[accessed 2026 October~21]{NEAcomp}. Around stars this bright, such planets are temperate and their masses are measurable with high-precision radial velocities. A single sector of the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite \citep[\tess;][]{Ricker2015}, about 27~d long, records at most one transit of such a planet, so finding them requires combining sectors observed years apart.

Stars observed only in full-frame images lie outside the two-minute transit search of the Science Processing Operations Center \citep[SPOC;][]{Jenkins2016}. Their long-period planets must be found in full-frame light curves, such as those of the Quick-Look Pipeline \citep[QLP;][]{Huang2020a, Huang2020b} and TESS-SPOC \citep{Caldwell2020}, which reach the most recent images only weeks to months after they are taken. \tic\ shows the difficulty. Until 2026, its public light curves held two transits four years apart, and the only intervening epoch that \tess\ observed fell on a scattered-light ramp, so the period could not be measured from public data.

Here we report a sub-Neptune candidate with a period of 36.34~days around \tic, a K dwarf at 67~pc. We found its first two transits in public full-frame light curves and fixed the period with our own photometry of the 2026 full-frame images. As of 2026 October~18, the star is neither a \tess\ Object of Interest \citep[TOI;][]{Guerrero2021} nor a community TOI, SPOC has reported no threshold-crossing event for it, and neither the Exoplanet Follow-up Observing Program (ExoFOP), the NASA Exoplanet Archive \citep{Akeson2013}, nor the NASA Astrophysics Data System lists planet parameters, follow-up observations, or publications for it. We call the candidate \planet, the designation ExoFOP gives the first community TOI on a star, and will submit it as a community TOI once this paper is published in a refereed journal, as ExoFOP requires.

\section{Host Star} \label{sec:star}

Table~\ref{tab:star} lists the properties of \tic. We adopt the stellar parameters of the \tess\ Input Catalog v8.2 \citep[TIC;][]{Stassun2019}. The \gaia\ DR3 \citep{Gaia2016, GaiaDR3} astrophysical parameters agree with them: the GSP-Phot effective temperature is 5089~K \citep{Andrae2023}, and the FLAME radius and mass, $0.843 \pm 0.018\,\rsun$ and $0.81 \pm 0.04\,\msun$ \citep{Creevey2023}, lie within $1\sigma$ of the TIC values. The FLAME radius would make the candidate 3\% larger.

\gaia\ DR3 reports a renormalized unit weight error \citep[RUWE;][]{Lindegren2021} of $1.00$, no multi-peaked image windows (\texttt{ipd\_frac\_multi\_peak} $= 0$), and no non-single-star solution, as expected for a single star. These indicators do not exclude a faint companion at small separation.

The light curves show no rotational modulation. The strongest Lomb--Scargle peaks of individual sectors lie between 5 and 13~d, differ from sector to sector, and stay below 0.1\% peak to peak in the TESS-SPOC light curves, whose systematics correction suppresses longer periods. We find no flares: no light curve has two or more consecutive cadences more than $4\sigma$ above its running median, except during one scattered-light episode in Sector~93 (BTJD~3830.6; \S\ref{sec:data}), when the excess follows the background. There is therefore no stellar signal against which to phase the transits.

\section{Observations and Photometry} \label{sec:data}

\tess\ observed \tic\ only in full-frame images, in Sectors~12 (2019), 39 (2021), 66 (2023), 93 (2025), and 100--103 (2026), at cadences of 1800~s in the prime mission, 600~s in the first extended mission, and 200~s thereafter. We use the TESS-SPOC light curves \citep[pre-search data conditioning flux;][]{Caldwell2020, TESSSPOC} of Sectors~12, 39, and~66 and the QLP light curve \citep[systematics-corrected flux;][]{Huang2020a, QLPdata} of Sector~93. For Sectors~100--103 we extracted light curves from $21 \times 21$-pixel cutouts \citep{Brasseur2019} of the SPOC-calibrated full-frame images \citep{TESSFFI}. The background of each cadence is the median of the faintest 40\% of pixels more than 4~pixels from the target. The aperture is a circle whose radius, chosen between 1 and 4~pixels in steps of 0.5, minimizes the one-hour scatter of the detrended light curve; the adopted radii are 1.5--2.5~pixels. We removed cadences flagged by SPOC and scattered-light glints, in which the background exceeds its one-day running median by more than eight times its median absolute deviation. QLP light curves of Sectors~100--103 were released at MAST between 2026 March and July but were absent from the public cloud copy of the archive that our search used. We use them only to check our timing (\S\ref{sec:ttv}).

The time stamps of the calibrated full-frame images are barycentric for a reference position on the detector, not for the target. Recomputing them for the target's position changes them by $-48$~s and $-20$~s at the Sector~100 and 102 transits. The TESS-SPOC and QLP light curves we fit already refer to the target: their time stamps precede those of the same cadences in the calibrated images by 20.7~s, in agreement with the 20--23~s we compute.

The TESS-SPOC fluxes are corrected for light from neighboring stars (\texttt{CROWDSAP} $= 0.92$--$0.94$); our photometry is not. The depths from the two kinds of light curve agree within their 4--7\% uncertainties (\S\ref{sec:times}), so any difference in dilution between them is smaller than the measurement precision.

For the transit analysis, we extract each transit in a window of $\pm 0.8$~d and normalize it by a quadratic function of time fitted to the out-of-transit data.

\section{Detection and Period} \label{sec:period}

We found the candidate in a search of 10,324,827 public light curves for individual transit-like events: QLP light curves of Sectors~1--98 and SPOC two-minute light curves \citep{TESSLC} of Sectors~99--107. We applied box filters of ten durations between 0.75 and 18~h and kept events above $6\sigma$ relative to the scatter of each filtered series. After we removed systematics shared by many stars, events at data edges and gaps, and non-planetary shapes, 2263 stars retained single transit-like events. We vetted the 142 of them brighter than $T = 10.5$ individually, at the pixel level and against catalogs of known objects, and searched the 27 that survived without a known period for further transits in our photometry of Sectors~99--108.

The public light curves of \tic\ contain two dips of matching depth and duration, in Sectors~39 and 93, 1453.58~d apart. Our photometry revealed two more transits, in Sectors~100 and 102, 36.36~d apart (Figure~\ref{fig:transits}). Because these two are consecutive, the period cannot be a multiple of 36.36~d, and only its integer fractions remain. We tested $P/2$ to $P/5$ by measuring the flux at every transit time they imply within the data, excluding the four observed transits. At $P/2$, transits are expected in @@N_P2@@ covered windows (Sectors~@@P2_SECTORS@@) and are absent from each at $\geq @@P2_MIN@@\sigma$; the other $P/2$ epochs in the observed sectors fall in the mid-sector gap of Sector~12, before the first good cadence of Sector~100, or after the end of the Sector~93 data. At $P/3$, transits are absent from @@N_P3@@ windows at $\geq @@P3_MIN@@\sigma$, and at $P/4$ and $P/5$ from @@N_P45@@ more at $\geq @@ALIAS_MIN@@\sigma$. At the adopted period, the Sector~12 data (BTJD 1624.9--1652.9, where BTJD $=$ BJD$_{\rm TDB} - 2457000$) fall between transits at BTJD~1621.5 and 1657.8, Sector~101 contains no transit, and the Sector~103 transit, at BTJD~4165.26, falls in the data gap between BTJD~4164.44 and 4166.79. A period of 36.34~d is therefore the only one consistent with every covered epoch.

The transit expected in Sector~66 (BTJD~3111.40) coincides with strong scattered light (Figure~\ref{fig:s66}). The background falls from $1.5 \times 10^4$ to $2.9 \times 10^3$~e$^-$~s$^{-1}$ across the window, the first cadences without quality flags begin @@S66_FIRST_H@@~h before the predicted mid-time (@@S66_PRE_INGRESS_H@@~h before ingress), and QLP flags every cadence within 12~h of it. The transit is covered, but the baseline before it is not. Fitted with the shape of \S\ref{sec:fit} and a free mid-time, the event has depths of @@S66_DEPTHS@@~ppm (uncertainties @@S66_DERR@@~ppm) for constant, linear, and quadratic baselines and lies @@S66_OC_RANGE@@~min from the linear ephemeris of \S\ref{sec:ttv}. Its timing is robust to the baseline, but its depth is not, so we exclude Sector~66 from all fits and from the ephemeris.

\section{Transit Analysis} \label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Individual Transit Times and Depths} \label{sec:times}

We timed each transit by fixing the shape to the median model of \S\ref{sec:fit} and scanning the mid-time on a 0.5-minute grid over $\pm 2$~h, refitting a quadratic baseline across the $\pm 0.8$~d window at each step. The $1\sigma$ uncertainty is the half-width of the $\Delta\chi^2 = 1$ interval multiplied by the red-noise factor $\beta$ \citep{Pont2006, Winn2008}, the median ratio of the binned residual scatter (10--60-minute bins) to its white-noise expectation. We measured each depth with the shape fixed and the amplitude free. Table~\ref{tab:times} lists the results.

The four depths agree ($\chi^2 = @@DEPTH_CHI2@@$ for 3 degrees of freedom), although they come from three independent reductions with different apertures, cadences, and crowding corrections. This agreement argues against an origin in any one pipeline and against aperture-dependent dilution. Their weighted mean is $@@DEPTH@@$~ppm.

\subsection{Transit Shape} \label{sec:fit}

We fitted the four transits jointly with \texttt{batman} \citep{Kreidberg2015}, integrating over each light curve's exposure time and centering each window on its measured mid-time so that the timing scatter (\S\ref{sec:ttv}) does not smear the stacked profile. The free parameters are $R_p/R_\star$, $b$, and $\log_{10}\rho_\star$, with uniform priors; $a/R_\star$ follows from $\rho_\star$ and $P$ for a circular orbit \citep{Seager2003}. The quadratic limb-darkening coefficients are fixed at $(u_1, u_2) = (0.39, 0.21)$, interpolated in $T_{\rm eff}$ from the \tess-band tables of \citet{Claret2017} at $\log g = 4.5$. Each window's flux uncertainty is its out-of-transit point-to-point scatter multiplied by its $\beta$. We sampled the posterior with \texttt{emcee} \citep{Goodman2010, ForemanMackey2013}, running 40 walkers for 30,000 steps and discarding the first third; the chain spans more than @@NTAU@@ integrated autocorrelation times for every parameter. Table~\ref{tab:params} gives the medians and 68\% intervals. The best-fitting model has a reduced $\chi^2$ of @@REDCHI2@@ for @@NPTS@@ data points, so the $\beta$-scaled uncertainties are slightly conservative.

The impact parameter is below @@B95@@ at 95\% confidence. The fitted stellar density, $@@RHO@@\,\rho_\odot$, agrees with the $1.61 \pm 0.38\,\rho_\odot$ derived from the TIC mass and radius, as expected for a circular orbit around a star of the cataloged density. Fitting the limb darkening as well, with the uninformative parametrization of \citet{Kipping2013}, changes $R_p/R_\star$ to @@K_FREELD@@ and $b$ to @@B_FREELD@@, both within $1\sigma$ of the adopted values.

\subsection{Ephemeris and Timing Residuals} \label{sec:ttv}

A weighted linear fit to the four mid-times gives $P = @@P@@$~d and $T_0 = @@T0@@$~BJD$_{\rm TDB}$, with the reference epoch at the Sector~93 transit, near the weighted mean epoch. The residuals (Table~\ref{tab:times}, Figure~\ref{fig:oc}) are @@OC_LIST@@~min. Their rms of @@RMS@@~min far exceeds the formal uncertainties of @@ERR_RANGE@@~min ($\chi^2 = @@EPH_CHI2@@$ for 2 degrees of freedom), and we scale the ephemeris uncertainties by $\sqrt{\chi^2/\nu}$. The largest single contribution is the interval between the consecutive Sector~100 and 102 transits, which exceeds $P$ by @@INTERVAL_EXCESS@@~min.

We looked for an instrumental cause. Changing the baseline (constant, linear, or quadratic) or the window half-width (0.6, 0.8, or 1.0~d) moves no mid-time by more than 2.3~min. The Sector~93 mid-time agrees within 1.9~min across every QLP flux series: the systematics-corrected flux, the raw flux, and the detrended fluxes of the optimal, small, and large apertures. That window is complete (688 of 691 cadences, no gap longer than 7~min) and shows no background or centroid feature at the transit. The time stamps of all four light curves refer to the target's barycentric position (\S\ref{sec:data}). Masking the transits of the 3.78-day signal (\S\ref{sec:second}), one of which falls 4~h before the Sector~93 transit, moves no mid-time by more than 0.1~min.

The QLP light curves of Sectors~100 and~102 provide an independent reduction of the same images, but their time stamps must first be corrected. Matched to the full-frame images by cadence number, they precede the mid-exposure times of the images, corrected to the target, by @@QLP_OFF100@@ and @@QLP_OFF102@@~s at the two transits; in Sector~93 the two agree within @@QLP_OFF93@@~s. The offset changes by @@QLP_SLOPES@@~s per day, the negatives of the rates at which the target's barycentric correction changes (@@BARY_RATES@@~s per day), and it vanishes near @@QLP_ZERO@@. The QLP times of Sectors~100 and~102 are also linear in cadence number to within @@QLP_LIN@@~s across each sector. Both results point to a single barycentric correction, equal to the target's near those epochs, applied to every cadence of each sector. QLP adopted the photometry of \citet{Han2023} from Sector~94 onward \citep{Petitpas2026}; the Sector~93 time stamps, which follow the barycentric correction, precede that change. We have reported the offset to the QLP team.

With this correction, and timed as in \S\ref{sec:times}, the QLP transits fall @@QLP_EARLY100@@ and @@QLP_EARLY102@@~min earlier than ours (@@QLP_SIG100@@ and @@QLP_SIG102@@ times the combined uncertainty), and the interval between them exceeds $P$ by @@QLP_INTERVAL@@~min, compared with @@INTERVAL_EXCESS@@~min in our photometry. Our photometry of the Sector~39 and 93 transits, reduced in the same way, gives mid-times within @@OWN_MAX@@~min of the TESS-SPOC and QLP values. The excess in the Sector~100--102 interval therefore does not arise in our reductions.

The QLP depths of the Sector~100 and 102 transits, $@@QLP_DEP100@@$ and $@@QLP_DEP102@@$~ppm, are lower than ours by @@QLP_DSIG100@@ and @@QLP_DSIG102@@ times the combined uncertainty, whereas the Sector~93 QLP depth, measured before the change of method, is $@@DEP93@@$~ppm and agrees with the others. Our photometry is not corrected for neighboring stars, which would make its depths too shallow rather than too deep, so the difference does not come from our treatment of crowding. We have not identified its cause, and neither QLP light curve enters our fits.

We identify no instrumental cause of the timing scatter in the light curves we fit. Four epochs cannot distinguish timing systematics from transit-timing variations induced by another body, and further transits will show whether the residuals are periodic. The predictions of \S\ref{sec:predictions} include the observed scatter.

\section{Vetting} \label{sec:vetting}

Without high-resolution imaging or spectroscopy, the light curves and the \gaia\ catalog test four things: whether the signal comes from the target's position (\S\ref{sec:centroid}), whether the one cataloged star close enough to contribute can produce it (\S\ref{sec:blend}), whether the target is itself an eclipsing binary (\S\ref{sec:secondary}), and how probable the remaining false-positive scenarios are, given the field (\S\ref{sec:fpp}). They cannot exclude an unresolved companion within about $1''$.

\subsection{Location of the Signal} \label{sec:centroid}

For each transit we formed a difference image (out of transit minus in transit) from the full-frame cutouts and fitted a Gaussian point-spread function to it \citep[cf.][]{Bryson2013, Twicken2018}. We measured each offset relative to the target's own fitted position in the out-of-transit image, which removes errors in the world-coordinate solution and the effect of proper motion. The four transits combined place the signal $1.5'' \pm 1.3''$ from \tic\ (Figure~\ref{fig:diff}). Applied to 40 confirmed \tess\ planets, the method placed 30 on their host star, left 5 unresolved from a nearby source, returned an offset without a cataloged counterpart for 3, failed for 2, and placed none on a neighboring star.

The centroid lies $1.2\sigma$ from the target and $1.6\sigma$ from one neighbor. Every other \gaia\ DR3 source bright enough to produce the observed depth lies $21\sigma$ or more away and is excluded; this one is not. The neighbor, \gaia\ DR3 5828441289812582656 ($G = 16.15$, $\Delta G = 6.37$), has a parallax of $0.69 \pm 0.08$~mas and is therefore in the background. Because of the target's proper motion, the separation grows from $3.10''$ at the \gaia\ epoch (2016.0) to $3.4''$--$3.6''$ during the transits.

\subsection{Blended Eclipsing Binary} \label{sec:blend}

Diluted by \tic, the neighbor could produce the observed depth only by losing about 74\% of its light in eclipse. We fitted the four transits with eclipse models of the neighbor for companion-to-star radius ratios $k$ from 0.3 to 10, with the impact parameter and $a/R_\star$ free, the dilution free but bounded at 0.4\% of the total flux (40\% above the star's share, to allow for its unknown \tess-band color), and three limb-darkening laws (Figure~\ref{fig:blend}). For $k \leq 1.2$, every eclipse model fits worse than the planet model by $\Delta\chi^2 \geq 142$ for 2227 data points. Larger companions fit better ($\Delta\chi^2 = 113$, 70, 44, 30, and 18 for $k = 1.5$, 2, 3, 5, and 10), because a total eclipse with a short ingress resembles a transit, but only if the eclipsed star supplies about 70\% of the neighbor's light and a companion 1.5 to 10 times its radius supplies the rest. The companion would then need a surface brightness 5 ($k = 1.5$) to 200 ($k = 10$) times lower than the eclipsed star's, which no main-sequence pair has; only a hot, compact eclipsed star could produce such an eclipse. A blend with the neighbor is therefore disfavored, and a ground-based light curve with an aperture that excludes the neighbor would settle the question.

\subsection{Secondary Eclipse and Odd--Even Depths} \label{sec:secondary}

The light curve shows no eclipse at orbital phase 0.5, which Sectors~39, 101, and~102 sample: the weighted depth there is $@@SEC_DEPTH@@$~ppm, below @@SEC_3SIG@@~ppm at $3\sigma$. A search over phases 0.3 to 0.7, which covers secondary eclipses of eccentric orbits with $|e\cos\omega| \lesssim 0.3$, finds no consistent signal. The $3\sigma$ upper limit is below 160~ppm at half of these phases and below 365~ppm at all of them. The most significant box, at phase 0.449 ($204 \pm 53$~ppm), is dominated by data from the last 0.5~d of Sector~93 and is not reproduced in Sectors~39 and 101. These limits exclude an eclipsing companion with a surface brightness comparable to the target's. Only one transit (Sector~100) falls on an odd epoch, so odd and even depths cannot be compared usefully, but the agreement of all four depths (\S\ref{sec:times}) disfavors an eclipsing binary at twice the period with unequal eclipses.

\subsection{Statistical False-Positive Probability} \label{sec:fpp}

Statistical validation with \texttt{TRICERATOPS} \citep{Giacalone2020, Giacalone2021} requires high-resolution imaging, and none exists for this star. Without a contrast curve, unresolved companions are constrained only by population priors, so the probabilities below do not validate the candidate. We computed scenario probabilities from the four transits stacked on their measured mid-times (10-minute bins), a depth of 2100~ppm, the photometric aperture of each sector, and the \gaia\ DR3 field. Ten independent runs give a false-positive probability ${\rm FPP} = 0.003 \pm 0.004$ (largest value 0.012) and a nearby false-positive probability ${\rm NFPP} < 10^{-6}$.\footnote{With the photometric apertures, NFPP is numerically zero ($<10^{-20}$); with the default $5 \times 5$-pixel apertures of \texttt{TRICERATOPS}, ${\rm FPP} = 0.001$ and ${\rm NFPP} = 6 \times 10^{-7}$.} A planet on the target receives 85\% of the probability and a planet diluted by an unresolved bound companion 14\%; the leading false-positive scenario, a planet orbiting an unresolved companion, receives 0.3\%. These values meet the numerical criteria of \citet{Giacalone2021}, ${\rm FPP} < 0.015$ and ${\rm NFPP} < 0.001$, but without imaging we classify \planet\ as a planet candidate.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

With $R_p = @@RP@@\,\rearth$, \planet\ lies above the radius valley \citep{Fulton2017}, among sub-Neptunes that probably retain a substantial volatile envelope. At $@@A_AU_SHORT@@$~AU it receives about ten times Earth's insolation. The mass--radius relation of \citet{ChenKipping2017} predicts $M_p \approx 14\,M_\oplus$, which would induce a radial-velocity semi-amplitude of about 2.8~m~s$^{-1}$, measurable with current high-precision spectrographs for a $V = 10.0$ star. For that mass, the transmission spectroscopy metric of \citet{Kempton2018} is about @@TSM@@, below the threshold of 90 that \citet{Kempton2018} recommend for sub-Neptunes, so the planet is a moderate rather than a prime target for transmission spectroscopy. If confirmed, it would be the second planet of 3--4\,$\rearth$ with a period longer than 30~days around a K dwarf brighter than $V = 10.5$, after TOI-2076~d.

Three observations would test the candidate most efficiently: high-resolution imaging, to exclude unresolved companions and complete the statistical validation; seeing-limited photometry of a transit with an aperture that excludes the $3.5''$ neighbor, to locate both signals; and radial velocities, to measure the mass. Each could overturn the present interpretation. A companion within $1''$ bright enough to dilute the transit would change the radius and the false-positive probability, a 74\% eclipse of the neighbor at the time of a transit would identify the signal as a blend, and a semi-amplitude far above the few meters per second expected for a sub-Neptune would reveal a stellar or substellar companion.
