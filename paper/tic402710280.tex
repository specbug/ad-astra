\title{A 36.34-day Sub-Neptune Candidate Transiting the Nearby K Dwarf TIC 402710280, Identified in TESS Full-Frame Images}

\author[0000-0000-0000-0000]{Rishit Vora}
\affiliation{Independent Researcher}
\email{EMAIL}

\begin{abstract}
We report a transiting sub-Neptune candidate with a period of 36.34 days around \tic\ (TYC~9041-03677-1), a K dwarf of \tess\ magnitude 9.24 at 66.8~pc that the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (\tess) has observed only in full-frame images. Four transits, recorded between 2021 and 2026 in Sectors~39, 93, 100 and~102 and reduced by three independent pipelines, share a depth of $2092 \pm 47$~ppm and a duration of $5.27 \pm 0.05$~h. Two of them fall on consecutive cycles, and every shorter period predicts transits in covered data that are absent at $\geq 18.6\sigma$; the period is $P = 36.33986 \pm 0.00051$~d. A transit model gives $R_p/R_\star = 0.0421^{+0.0007}_{-0.0005}$ and a stellar density consistent with the host's. The candidate has a radius of $3.75 \pm 0.22\,\rearth$ and an equilibrium temperature of $498 \pm 21$~K. Difference-image centroids place the signal $1.5'' \pm 1.3''$ from the target. The only catalogued star close enough to contribute, a background star $3.5''$ away, would have to host an eclipsing binary whose eclipse shape is disfavoured for any main-sequence pair ($\Delta\chi^2 \geq 142$). No secondary eclipse is detected, and \texttt{TRICERATOPS} gives a false-positive probability of $0.003 \pm 0.004$. No high-resolution imaging exists, so companions within about $1''$ are constrained only by \gaia\ astrometry. The mid-times scatter about a linear ephemeris by 21.8~min (rms), which we report without choosing between an instrumental and a dynamical origin. The light curve also contains a $407 \pm 26$~ppm signal with a period of 3.78~days whose source, the target or the background star, the present data cannot identify. We give predicted transit times, including a transit in \tess\ Sector~114 on 2027 February~19.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}

Sub-Neptunes with orbital periods longer than a month around bright K dwarfs are rare among known transiting planets, yet they are the temperate members of the population for which both precise masses and atmospheric spectra are within reach: the stars are bright enough for high-precision radial velocities, and their small radii deepen both the transit and its atmospheric signal. A single sector of the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite \citep[\tess;][]{Ricker2015}, about 27~d long, records at most one transit of such a planet. Finding them therefore means combining sectors observed years apart, which became possible only as the full-frame images accumulated.

Stars observed only in full-frame images lie outside the Science Processing Operations Center (SPOC) search of two-minute data \citep{Jenkins2016}. Their long-period planets must be found in full-frame light curves, such as those of the Quick-Look Pipeline \citep[QLP;][]{Huang2020a, Huang2020b} and TESS-SPOC \citep{Caldwell2020}, and in photometry of the most recent images, which these products do not yet cover. The star studied here illustrates the difficulty: until 2026 its public light curves contained two transits four years apart, and the one predicted between them fell on a scattered-light ramp, so the period could not be measured from public data.

Here we report a sub-Neptune candidate with a period of 36.34~days around \tic, a K dwarf at 67~pc. Its first two transits were identified in public full-frame light curves; the period was determined from our photometry of the 2026 full-frame images. As of 2026 October~14, the star is neither a \tess\ Object of Interest \citep[TOI;][]{Guerrero2021} nor a community TOI, and no planet parameters, follow-up observations or literature references are associated with it in the Exoplanet Follow-up Observing Program (ExoFOP) archive, the NASA Exoplanet Archive \citep{Akeson2013}, or the NASA Astrophysics Data System. We refer to the candidate as \planet, the designation ExoFOP assigns to the first community TOI on a star; we will submit it as a community TOI upon publication in a refereed journal, as ExoFOP requires for new candidates.

\section{Host Star} \label{sec:star}

Table~\ref{tab:star} lists the properties of \tic. We adopt the stellar parameters of the \tess\ Input Catalog v8.2 \citep[TIC;][]{Stassun2019}. The \gaia\ DR3 \citep{Gaia2016, GaiaDR3} astrophysical parameters agree with them: the GSP-Phot effective temperature is 5089~K \citep{Andrae2023}, and the FLAME radius and mass are $0.843 \pm 0.018\,\rsun$ and $0.81 \pm 0.04\,\msun$ \citep{Creevey2023}, within $1\sigma$ of the TIC values. Adopting the FLAME radius would increase the radius of the candidate by 3\%.

\gaia\ DR3 reports a renormalised unit weight error \citep[RUWE;][]{Lindegren2021} of $1.00$, no multi-peaked image windows (\texttt{ipd\_frac\_multi\_peak} $= 0$), and no non-single-star solution. These indicate a single, well-behaved astrometric source, although they do not exclude a companion at small separation and low mass ratio.

The light curves show no coherent rotational modulation. The strongest Lomb--Scargle peaks of the individual sectors lie between 5 and 13~d, differ from sector to sector, and do not exceed 0.1\% peak to peak in the TESS-SPOC light curves, whose systematics correction suppresses longer periods. No light curve contains two or more consecutive cadences more than $4\sigma$ above its running median, except during one scattered-light episode in Sector~93 (BTJD~3830.6, see \S\ref{sec:data}), where the excess follows the background; we find no flares. No stellar signal is therefore available with which the transits could be phased.

\section{Observations and Photometry} \label{sec:data}

\tess\ observed \tic\ in Sectors~12 (2019), 39 (2021), 66 (2023), 93 (2025) and 100--103 (2026), always in full-frame images, at cadences of 1800~s (prime mission), 600~s (first extended mission) and 200~s thereafter. We use the TESS-SPOC light curves \citep{Caldwell2020, TESSSPOC} for Sectors~12, 39 and~66 (pre-search data conditioning flux) and the QLP light curve \citep{Huang2020a, QLPdata} for Sector~93 (systematics-corrected flux). Neither pipeline had processed Sectors~100--103 when this work was done, so we extracted their light curves from the SPOC-calibrated full-frame images, using $21 \times 21$-pixel cutouts \citep{Brasseur2019}. The background of each cadence is the median of the faintest 40\% of pixels more than 4~pixels from the target. The aperture is a circle whose radius, between 1 and 4~pixels in steps of 0.5, minimises the one-hour scatter of the detrended light curve; the adopted radii are 1.5--2.5~pixels. Cadences flagged by SPOC are removed, as are scattered-light glints, in which the background exceeds its one-day running median by more than eight times its median absolute deviation.

The time stamps of the calibrated full-frame images are barycentric for a reference position on the detector rather than for the target. We recomputed them for the target's position, a correction of $-48$~s and $-20$~s at the Sector~100 and 102 transits. The TESS-SPOC and QLP light curves are corrected for the target already: their time stamps precede those of the same cadences in the calibrated images by 20.7~s, in agreement with the 20--23~s we compute.

The TESS-SPOC fluxes are corrected for the flux of neighbouring stars (\texttt{CROWDSAP} $= 0.92$--$0.94$); we apply no such correction to our photometry. The depths measured in the two kinds of light curve agree within their 4--7\% uncertainties (\S\ref{sec:times}), so any difference in dilution between the reductions is smaller than the measurement precision.

For transit analysis, each event is extracted in a window of $\pm 0.8$~d and normalised by a quadratic function of time fitted to the out-of-transit data.

\section{Detection and Period} \label{sec:period}

The candidate was found in a search of 10\,324\,827 public light curves (QLP light curves of Sectors~1--98 and SPOC two-minute light curves of nine sectors) for individual transit-like events. Box filters of ten durations between 0.75 and 18~h were applied, with a detection threshold of $6\sigma$ relative to the scatter of each filtered series. After removal of systematics shared by many stars, events at data edges and gaps, and non-planetary shapes, 2263 stars retained single transit-like events. The 142 of these brighter than $T = 10.5$ were vetted individually at the pixel level and against catalogues of known objects, and the 27 that survived without a known period were searched for further transits in our photometry of Sectors~99--108.

For \tic\ the public data contain two dips of matching depth and duration, in Sectors~39 and 93, separated by 1453.58~d. Our photometry revealed two further transits, in Sectors~100 and 102, separated by 36.36~d (Figure~\ref{fig:transits}). Because these two transits are consecutive, the period cannot be a multiple of 36.36~d; the remaining possibilities are its integer fractions. We tested $P/2$ through $P/5$ by measuring the flux at every transit time they predict within the data, excluding the four observed events. $P/2$ predicts transits at three covered epochs (Sectors~39, 101 and~102), each absent at $\geq 28.5\sigma$; $P/3$ predicts eight, absent at $\geq 27.8\sigma$; and $P/4$ and $P/5$ predict 30 more, of which the least significant exclusion is $18.6\sigma$. The adopted period predicts no transit in Sector~12, whose data (BTJD 1624.9--1652.9, where BTJD $=$ BJD$_{\rm TDB} - 2457000$) fall between predicted transits at BTJD~1621.5 and 1657.8, and none in Sector~101; its Sector~103 transit, at BTJD~4165.26, falls in the data gap between BTJD~4164.44 and 4166.79. A period of 36.34~d is therefore the only one consistent with the covered epochs.

The transit predicted in Sector~66 (BTJD~3111.40) coincides with an interval of strong scattered light (Figure~\ref{fig:s66}). The background falls from $1.5 \times 10^4$ to $2.9 \times 10^3$~e$^-$~s$^{-1}$ across the window, quality-flagged cadences end only 3.2~h before the predicted mid-time (0.6~h before ingress), and QLP flags every cadence within 12~h of it. A transit model fitted at the predicted time returns a depth of $1708 \pm 271$~ppm, consistent with the other events, but the result depends on the treatment of the one-sided baseline. We therefore exclude Sector~66 from all fits; it neither supports nor contradicts the candidate.

\section{Transit Analysis} \label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Individual Transit Times and Depths} \label{sec:times}

Each transit is timed by fixing the shape to the median model of \S\ref{sec:fit} and scanning the mid-time on a 0.5-minute grid over $\pm 2$~h, refitting a quadratic baseline over the $\pm 0.8$~d window at each step. The $1\sigma$ uncertainty is the half-width of the $\Delta\chi^2 = 1$ interval multiplied by the red-noise factor $\beta$ \citep{Pont2006, Winn2008}, the median ratio of the binned residual scatter (10--60-minute bins) to its white-noise expectation. The depth of each event is measured with the shape fixed and a free amplitude. Table~\ref{tab:times} lists the results.

The four depths agree ($\chi^2 = 2.5$ for 3 degrees of freedom) although they come from three independent reductions with different apertures, cadences and crowding treatments. The agreement argues against an origin in any one pipeline and against aperture-dependent dilution. Their weighted mean is $2092 \pm 47$~ppm.

\subsection{Transit Shape} \label{sec:fit}

We model the four transits jointly with \texttt{batman} \citep{Kreidberg2015}, integrating over each light curve's exposure time and centring each window on its measured mid-time, so that timing scatter (\S\ref{sec:ttv}) does not smear the stacked profile. The free parameters are $R_p/R_\star$, $b$ and $\log_{10}\rho_\star$, with uniform priors; $a/R_\star$ follows from $\rho_\star$ and $P$ \citep{Seager2003} for a circular orbit. Quadratic limb-darkening coefficients are fixed at $(u_1, u_2) = (0.39, 0.21)$, interpolated in $T_{\rm eff}$ from the \tess-band tables of \citet{Claret2017} at $\log g = 4.5$. Each window's flux uncertainty is its out-of-transit point-to-point scatter multiplied by its $\beta$. We sample the posterior with \texttt{emcee} \citep{Goodman2010, ForemanMackey2013}, using 40 walkers for 30\,000 steps and discarding the first third; the chain spans more than 90 integrated autocorrelation times for every parameter. Table~\ref{tab:params} gives the medians and 68\% intervals. The best-fitting model has a reduced $\chi^2$ of 0.84 (2227 data points), so the $\beta$-scaled uncertainties are slightly conservative.

The impact parameter is below 0.54 at 95\% confidence. The fitted stellar density, $1.57^{+0.12}_{-0.31}\,\rho_\odot$, agrees with the value of $1.61 \pm 0.38\,\rho_\odot$ derived from the TIC mass and radius; the transit is therefore consistent with a circular orbit around a star of the catalogued density. Fitting the limb darkening as well, with the uninformative parametrisation of \citet{Kipping2013}, changes $R_p/R_\star$ to $0.0426 \pm 0.0006$ and $b$ to $0.21^{+0.19}_{-0.15}$, both within $1\sigma$ of the adopted values.

\subsection{Ephemeris and Timing Residuals} \label{sec:ttv}

A weighted linear fit to the four mid-times gives $P = 36.33986 \pm 0.00051$~d and $T_0 = 2460838.2008 \pm 0.0119$~BJD$_{\rm TDB}$, with the reference epoch at the Sector~93 transit, near the weighted mean epoch. The residuals (Table~\ref{tab:times}, Figure~\ref{fig:oc}) are $+4.4$, $-23.2$, $+6.8$ and $+35.9$~min, with an rms of 21.8~min, much larger than the 1.7--3.3-min formal uncertainties ($\chi^2 = 417$ for 2 degrees of freedom). We scale the ephemeris uncertainties by $\sqrt{\chi^2/\nu}$. The largest single contribution is the interval between the consecutive Sector~100 and 102 transits, which exceeds $P$ by 29.2~min.

We searched for an instrumental cause. Varying the baseline (constant, linear or quadratic) and the window half-width (0.6, 0.8 or 1.0~d) moves no mid-time by more than 2.3~min. The Sector~93 time is the same, within 1.9~min, in every QLP flux series (the systematics-corrected flux, the raw flux, and the detrended fluxes of the optimal, small and large apertures), and that window is complete (688 of 691 cadences, no gap longer than 7~min), with no background or centroid feature at the transit. The time stamps of all four light curves refer to the target's barycentric position (\S\ref{sec:data}). Masking the transits of the 3.78-day signal (\S\ref{sec:second}), one of which falls 4~h before the Sector~93 transit, changes no mid-time by more than 0.1~min. We therefore identify no instrumental cause, and four epochs cannot distinguish timing systematics from transit-timing variations induced by an additional body. Additional transits will show whether the residuals are periodic. The predictions in \S\ref{sec:predictions} include the observed scatter.

\section{Vetting} \label{sec:vetting}

Without high-resolution imaging or spectroscopy, the light curves and the \gaia\ catalogue can test four things: whether the signal comes from the target's position (\S\ref{sec:centroid}), whether the one catalogued star near enough to contribute can produce it (\S\ref{sec:blend}), whether the target is itself an eclipsing binary (\S\ref{sec:secondary}), and how probable the remaining false-positive scenarios are given the field (\S\ref{sec:fpp}). They cannot exclude an unresolved companion closer than about $1''$.

\subsection{Location of the Signal} \label{sec:centroid}

For each transit we form a difference image (out-of-transit minus in-transit) from the full-frame image cutouts and fit a Gaussian point-spread function to it \citep[cf.][]{Bryson2013, Twicken2018}. Each offset is measured relative to the target's own fitted position in the out-of-transit image, which removes errors in the image's world-coordinate solution and the effect of proper motion. Combining the four transits gives an offset of $1.5'' \pm 1.3''$ from \tic\ (Figure~\ref{fig:diff}). We validated the method on 40 confirmed \tess\ planets: it placed 30 on their host star, left 5 unresolved from a nearby source, returned an offset without a catalogued counterpart for 3, failed for 2, and placed none on a neighbouring star.

The centroid is $1.2\sigma$ from the target and $1.6\sigma$ from one neighbour: it excludes every other \gaia\ DR3 source bright enough to produce the observed depth, each of which lies $21\sigma$ or more away, but not this one. The neighbour, \gaia\ DR3 5828441289812582656 ($G = 16.15$, $\Delta G = 6.37$), has a parallax of $0.69 \pm 0.08$~mas, which places it in the background. Owing to the target's proper motion, its separation increases from $3.10''$ at the \gaia\ epoch (2016.0) to $3.4''$--$3.6''$ during the transits.

\subsection{Blended Eclipsing Binary} \label{sec:blend}

Diluted by \tic, the neighbour could reproduce the observed depth only through an eclipse of about 74\% of its own light. We fitted the four transits with eclipse models of the neighbour for companion-to-star radius ratios $k$ from 0.3 to 10, with the impact parameter and $a/R_\star$ free, the dilution free but bounded at 0.4\% of the total flux (40\% above the star's share, to allow for its unknown \tess-band colour), and three limb-darkening laws (Figure~\ref{fig:blend}). For $k \leq 1.2$ every eclipse model is worse than the planet model by $\Delta\chi^2 \geq 142$ for 2227 data points. Larger companions fit better ($\Delta\chi^2 = 113$, 70, 44, 30 and 18 for $k = 1.5$, 2, 3, 5 and 10), because a total eclipse with a short ingress resembles a transit, but only if the eclipsed star supplies about 70\% of the neighbour's light while a companion 1.5 to 10 times its radius supplies the rest. The companion's surface brightness would then have to be below about 20\% ($k = 1.5$) to 0.5\% ($k = 10$) of the eclipsed star's, which no main-sequence pair satisfies; only a hot, compact eclipsed star could. A blend with the neighbour is therefore disfavoured, and a ground-based light curve with an aperture that excludes it would settle the question directly.

\subsection{Secondary Eclipse and Odd--Even Depths} \label{sec:secondary}

The light curve at orbital phase 0.5, sampled in Sectors~39, 101 and~102, shows no eclipse: the weighted depth is $-39 \pm 39$~ppm, below 77~ppm at $3\sigma$. A search over phases 0.3 to 0.7, which covers secondary eclipses of eccentric orbits with $|e\cos\omega| \lesssim 0.3$, finds no consistent signal: the 3$\sigma$ upper limit is below 160~ppm at half of the phases and below 365~ppm at all of them. The most significant box, at phase 0.449 ($204 \pm 53$~ppm), is dominated by data within 0.5~d of the end of Sector~93 and is not reproduced in Sectors~39 and 101. These limits exclude an eclipsing companion of comparable surface brightness to the target. Only one of the four transits (Sector~100) falls on an odd epoch, so a comparison of odd and even depths is not informative; the agreement of all four depths (\S\ref{sec:times}) nonetheless disfavours an eclipsing binary at twice the period with unequal eclipses.

\subsection{Statistical False-Positive Probability} \label{sec:fpp}

Statistical validation with \texttt{TRICERATOPS} \citep{Giacalone2020, Giacalone2021} requires high-resolution imaging to constrain unresolved companions, and none exists for this star; the probabilities below are therefore upper bounds on the evidence and do not validate the candidate. We computed scenario probabilities using the four transits stacked on their measured mid-times (10-minute bins), a depth of 2100~ppm, the photometric aperture of each sector, and the \gaia\ DR3 field. Ten independent runs give a false-positive probability ${\rm FPP} = 0.003 \pm 0.004$ (largest value 0.012) and a nearby false-positive probability ${\rm NFPP} < 10^{-6}$.\footnote{With the photometric apertures NFPP is numerically zero ($<10^{-20}$); with \texttt{TRICERATOPS}'s default $5 \times 5$-pixel apertures, ${\rm FPP} = 0.001$ and ${\rm NFPP} = 6 \times 10^{-7}$.} The planet hypothesis on the target carries 85\% of the probability and a planet diluted by an unresolved bound companion 14\%; the leading false-positive scenario, a planet orbiting an unresolved companion, carries 0.3\%. These values satisfy the numerical criteria of \citet{Giacalone2021}, ${\rm FPP} < 0.015$ and ${\rm NFPP} < 0.001$, but without imaging we classify \planet\ as a planet candidate.

\section{A Second Periodic Signal} \label{sec:second}

With the transits of \planet\ masked, a box-least-squares search \citep{Kovacs2002} of the light curve finds a second periodic signal, with $P = 3.78371$~d, a depth of $407 \pm 26$~ppm and a duration of about 1.3~h (Figure~\ref{fig:second}). It is detected independently in Sectors~12--93, as the strongest periodicity (signal detection efficiency 14.4), and in Sectors~100--103, as the second strongest after a period five times longer; the two ephemerides agree in phase across 650 cycles. The depths in the eight sectors agree with one another ($\chi^2 = 7.3$ for 7 degrees of freedom; Figure~\ref{fig:second}), the odd and even transits agree ($382 \pm 39$ and $437 \pm 46$~ppm), and no eclipse is seen at phase 0.5 ($-61 \pm 26$~ppm).

A transit model with free stellar density gives $R_p/R_\star = 0.0197^{+0.0018}_{-0.0009}$, $b = 0.50 \pm 0.33$ and $\rho_\star = 8.2^{+3.7}_{-5.9}\,\rho_\odot$. On the target, the signal would correspond to a planet of $1.75 \pm 0.12\,\rearth$ on an inner orbit. The fitted density, $8^{+4}_{-6}\,\rho_\odot$, exceeds the target's $1.61 \pm 0.38\,\rho_\odot$: a transit across the target reproduces the short duration only at a high impact parameter, near the upper end of the 68\% interval of $b$ (0.16--0.83). The difference-image centroid of this weaker signal ($5.2'' \pm 6.7''$ from the target) cannot separate the target from the background neighbour, on which it would require an eclipse of about 14\% of the neighbour's light.  We therefore do not claim this signal as a planet candidate. A ground-based light curve that resolves the neighbour would show a 14\% eclipse on it if it is the source.

\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

With $R_p = 3.75 \pm 0.22\,\rearth$, \planet\ lies above the radius valley \citep{Fulton2017}, among sub-Neptunes that probably retain a substantial volatile envelope. At $0.206$~AU it receives ten times Earth's insolation. The mass--radius relation of \citet{ChenKipping2017} predicts $M_p \approx 14\,M_\oplus$ and a radial-velocity semi-amplitude of $K \approx 2.8$~m~s$^{-1}$, within reach of current high-precision spectrographs for a $V = 10.0$ star.

Three observations would most efficiently test the candidate: high-resolution imaging, to exclude unresolved companions and complete the statistical validation; seeing-limited photometry of a transit with an aperture that excludes the $3.5''$ neighbour, which would locate both signals; and radial velocities, to measure the mass. Each could overturn the present interpretation. A companion within $1''$ bright enough to dilute the transit would change the radius and the false-positive probability; a 74\% eclipse of the neighbour at the time of a transit would identify the signal as a blend; and a semi-amplitude far above the few metres per second expected for a sub-Neptune would indicate a stellar or substellar companion.
