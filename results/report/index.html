Amateur search of NASA TESS data · survey report · 15 October 2026

Ad Astra

A blind search of 10.3 million TESS light curves for transits and eclipses that no catalogue lists yet, extended with our own light curves of the newest TESS images and with Gaia, ZTF and KELT data. Checked against ExoFOP, SIMBAD, VizieR, NASA's archive and the literature, most of what it found is already known to someone. What is left: a sub-Neptune candidate every 36.3 days around the nearby K dwarf TIC 402710280, which is on no list; the 79-day period of TOI-2493.01, a temperate sub-Neptune candidate; the period of a sub-Neptune around HIP 56966; a second transit of a small planet around HD 11506; 19 single transits on bright stars that nobody has posted; and corrections to ExoFOP: a community candidate whose period is half the posted one, three single transits merged into TOIs they do not belong to, and TOI ephemerides that no longer find their transits.

Summary

What the search found

  • New candidate
    TIC 402710280: a 3.8 R⊕ planet candidate every 36.340 days around a K dwarf 67 parsecs away (TESS magnitude 9.2). Not a TOI, not a community TOI, nothing on record anywhere. Four transits from 2021 to 2026 (a fifth falls on stray light), two of them only in our own light curves of the newest TESS full-frame images; the dip comes from the star or from within 2″ of it, and has a planet's shape.
  • Period new
    TOI-2493.01: a 2.7 R⊕ candidate every 79.03 days, receiving 1.3× Earth's insolation. A single-transit TOI since 2021 with no period; the period hunt found two more transits in 2025 and 2026. TESS should see the next one in mid-November 2026.
  • New period
    HIP 56966 (HD 101544): a 3.1 R⊕ planet candidate every 19.826 days around a V = 8.8 Sun-like star. Its 2019 transit was posted to ExoFOP in 2025 as a single-transit community candidate (Salinas et al.), with no period. This survey found three more transits across seven years and the period they share.
  • Second transit
    HD 11506: a 3.8 R⊕ transiting candidate in a known three-planet system. The 2020 transit was posted as a single-transit community candidate in 2024, but with a depth and duration ten times too large. This survey measures it properly and finds a second transit in August 2026, 2,149 days later, so the period is 25.9 days or longer. The star's three giant planets, found by radial velocities, do not fit.
  • Found again
    HIP 71639: a 4.1 R⊕ planet candidate every 23.884 days around a V = 6 F star. Planet Hunters TESS posted its first transit in 2020, and another ExoFOP user posted this period from the same four transits in May 2026. The survey found it independently, five months later, and agrees within 0.1σ. It is still not a TOI.
  • 19 bright
    19 transit candidates on bright stars that nobody has posted. Of 142 shortlisted stars brighter than T = 10.5, 34 pass the pixel, period and binarity tests; 15 of those were already on record, as community candidates, confirmed planets (HD 3167, HD 278555, K2-290), binaries, or once a transit of HD 80606 b on the neighbouring star. The 19 left are mostly single transits of giant planets; the smallest are 5.8 R⊕ (TIC 155169961), 5.8 R⊕ (TIC 393876901), 6.9 R⊕ (TIC 441010857). Most of the rest fail the pixel test: their dips come from fainter neighbours.
  • None new
    Triple and quadruple stars: every unpublished one is a blend. Of the 30 strongest of 251 stars with two independent, recurring eclipse sets, the six whose eclipses all come from the target are already published quadruples or a sextuple. In every other one, a pixel test traces one eclipse set to a neighbouring star, and ZTF's sharper images see those eclipses on the named neighbour wherever they reach.
  • 126 periods
    Periods for TOIs and community TOIs that have none. All 1003 were searched in every TESS sector, including our own light curves of sectors 99–108; 390 gained transits that pass the checks. Where someone had already posted a period for the same candidate, the hunt agrees in 33 of 44 cases, and five of the others turn out to be errors in the posted value; 126 periods, narrowed to three or fewer choices, are on no list (TOI-2493.01 is the best of them).
  • Corrections
    Five ExoFOP entries that the TESS data contradict. Community TOI 438225366.01 has a period of 20.862 days, half the posted 41.72; TOI-2633.01's transits fall every 18.215 days from a different epoch than its listed ephemeris, which predicts transits where the light curve is flat; and three single-transit community TOIs merged into later TOIs (TOI-7602.01, 5967.01, 6033.01) are separate objects, each with a further transit found here.
  • 41 ephemerides
    TOIs whose catalogued ephemeris no longer finds their transits. Every planet-candidate TOI was checked against all TESS sectors: for 41, the ExoFOP ephemeris misses the transits by more than a transit duration and more than three of its own error bars, and nobody has posted a better one; corrected ephemerides from the individual transit times are given for them and for 214 more whose listed errors are merely large.
  • Measured
    The false-alarm floor, and what was found again. Flipped light curves produced no planet-shaped dips in two-minute data. Blind, the survey found nine confirmed planetary systems that never became TOIs, 83 community candidates, published quadruple candidates, a sextuple star and a dipper.

TIC 402710280: a sub-Neptune every 36.3 days

Not a TOINot a community TOIPeriod newNothing on record

A K dwarf (TESS magnitude 9.2, V ≈ 10) 67 parsecs away in Triangulum Australe dims by 2101 ppm for 5.3 hours every 36.3399 days: a planet about 3.8 times the size of Earth. The survey first saw two of its transits, 1,454 days apart; our own light curves of the newest full-frame images found two more on consecutive orbits (sectors 100 and 102), which fix the period. A fifth predicted transit, in sector 66, falls on a stray-light ramp and cannot be judged.

Vetting

  • ✓Passed:
    Four transits of one depth. Sectors 39 (TESS-SPOC), 93 (QLP), 100 and 102 (our own full-frame photometry): depths 2203 ± 85, 2050 ± 82, 2075 ± 150, 2045 ± 86 ppm, mean 2097 ± 46 (χ² 2.3 for 3 degrees of freedom): three independent pipelines see the same dip. Every shorter alias predicts transits inside the data that are not there (sector 101 is flat at P/2 and P/3); sector 103's falls in a data gap.
  • !Caution:
    Sector 66 cannot tell. The transit predicted in sector 66 falls on a stray-light ramp: TESS-SPOC's good data start 3.2 h before mid-transit (0.6 h before ingress), the background falls from 15,000 to 2,900 e⁻/s across the window, the raw flux sags for 13 hours, and QLP flags every cadence within 12 hours. A transit fit gives 1620 ± 200 ppm at the predicted time, but the answer depends on the baseline, so the sector counts neither for nor against. An earlier version of this report counted it as a fifth transit and fitted it; that inflated the agreement and biased the depth low (1655 ppm).
  • !Caution:
    Transit times scatter. Measured one by one (shape fixed, baseline refitted, red-noise errors), the mid-times sit S39 +5 ± 2, S93 -25 ± 2, S100 +6 ± 3, S102 +34 ± 2 min from a straight-line ephemeris (χ² = 461 for 2 degrees of freedom). Either the timing is disturbed by systematics (the sector-102 transit lies on a ramp near an orbit start) or the planet shows timing variations; four times cannot tell. The period and epoch errors below are inflated to match, and predictions carry about ±20 minutes; the ±2 minutes quoted earlier was wrong.
  • ✓Passed:
    The signal is on this star, or within 2″ of it. Difference-image centroids of the four transits, measured from the star's own image on each frame: 1.5″ ± 1.3″ from the target. Every Gaia star able to fake the dip lies 22σ or more from that position, except one: a G = 16.2 background star 2.2″ away (parallax 0.69 mas against the target's 15.0), which centroids cannot separate.
  • ✓Passed:
    Not that background star, by shape. Diluted by the target (ΔG = 6.4), a 2097 ppm dip needs an eclipse of about 74% of that star's light: a companion nearly as large as the star, with long ingress and egress. Fitted as such an eclipse (radius ratio 0.3 to 1.2, three limb-darkening laws, the star given 40% more light than it has), the four transits fit worse than a planet by Δχ² ≥ 142 (2,227 points, reduced χ² of the planet fit 0.84). Strongly disfavoured; only high-resolution imaging excludes it.
  • ✓Passed:
    A transit this star can make. The shape, fitted with each transit centred on its own mid-time, gives a stellar density of 1.56+0.12−0.33 solar against the catalogue K dwarf's 1.61, impact parameter 0.23 ± 0.20. (Forcing all transits onto one straight-line ephemeris smears the shape; that, not the sector-102 ramp, is what made earlier fits look grazing.)
  • ✓Passed:
    No secondary eclipse. Half an orbit later the light curve is flat (37 to 120 ± 48 ppm). Odd and even transits cannot be compared: the four fall on cycles −30, 10, 17 and 18, so only one (sector 100) is odd.
  • ✓Passed:
    No sign of a stellar companion. Gaia DR3: RUWE 1.00, no non-single-star solution, a single image in every scan.
  • ✓Passed:
    TRICERATOPS: FPP 0.003, NFPP 4e-24. TRICERATOPS 1.1 (Giacalone et al. 2021) on the four clean transits (each centred on its measured mid-time, 10-minute bins, depth 2100 ppm), with each sector's photometric aperture and the Gaia field: false-positive probability 0.003 ± 0.004 over 10 runs, nearby-star FPP 4e-24. A planet on this star carries 85% of the probability; the main false-positive scenario (0.3%) is a planet orbiting an unseen companion star. Check: with TRICERATOPS's larger default 5×5-pixel apertures, FPP 0.001 and NFPP 6e-07. These values are below the validation thresholds (FPP < 0.015, NFPP < 0.001), but without a contrast curve from high-resolution imaging this is not a validation. The first run (sector 66 included, 1449 ppm depth) gave FPP 0.015 and is withdrawn.
  • iNote:
    Half the transits only in our own photometry. The sector 100 and 102 transits, and the flat sector 101 that rules out shorter periods, come from our own photometry of the full-frame images (ffi_new.py: 15×15-pixel cutouts of the calibrated images, a circular aperture, a local background annulus); no public light curve of those sectors exists yet (the public QLP light curves end at sector 98). Sector 100's scatter is 1.5 times its white-noise expectation, which its errors here include.
  • iNote:
    On record: nothing. Not a TOI or community TOI, no planet parameters, imaging or spectra on ExoFOP, and absent from the full TOI and community-TOI tables (checked live on 14 October 2026); not in SIMBAD's object types, the NASA archive or the literature searches. SPOC never observed it at 2-minute cadence, so it has no SPOC TCE.

Not a TOIPeriod newCommunity TOI since 2025, no period

A Sun-like G0 star (V = 8.8), 73 parsecs away in Centaurus, dims by 775 ppm for 2.8 hours every 19.8262 days. A planet about 3.1 times the size of Earth would do this. SPOC never saw two of its transits in one search, so it raised no alert.

Vetting

  • ✓Passed:
    Four transits, one period. Dips in sectors 10, 64, 99 and 100 (710, 651, 694 and 799 ppm, each ±60) fall on one ephemeris with 4 minutes rms: 125 and 51 cycles separate the sector-10 and sector-64 transits from the sector-99 one. Every shorter alias predicts transits inside the data that are missing, ruled out at more than 16σ. The only other predicted transit since 2019 fell in the mid-sector gap of sector 37.
  • ✓Passed:
    No secondary eclipse. Half an orbit after each transit the light curves are flat to about 150 ppm, as for a planet and unlike most eclipsing binaries.
  • !Caution:
    Probably on this star; the offset is marginal. Difference-image centroids of the transits (2-minute pixels), measured from the star's own image on each frame: 6.9″ ± 2.5″ from the star, a 2.7σ offset, under the 3σ line but not comfortably. The faint stars that could fake the dip lie 5.8σ and 7.7σ from that position (G15.7 at 18", G15.1 at 15"; they would need eclipses over 50% deep). The sector-64 transit, seen only in full-frame images, gives a discordant centroid (23″ off, blended in the large full-frame pixels) and is left out of the centroid but kept for timing and depth. A source closer than about 7″ that Gaia does not list is not excluded.
  • ✓Passed:

  • !Caution:
    Slightly short transit. A circular orbit at this period gives a 2.8 ± 0.1-hour transit if the planet crosses at impact parameter ≈ 0.87; the shape prefers 0.51 ± 0.36. Consistent within 1σ, or a modest eccentricity.

HD 11506: a fourth planet in a known planetary system?

Not a TOISecond transit newCommunity TOI since 2024Host has 3 RV planets

HD 11506 is a bright Sun-like star in Cetus with three giant planets found by the radial-velocity method, none known to transit. TESS caught two matching 3.2-hour dips, almost six years apart, of the size a planet 3.8 times the size of Earth would make.

What the period could be

The transits are 2149.39 days apart, so the period is that gap divided by a whole number. Each choice predicts other transits; where TESS was watching at those times (sectors 3, 30 and 107, and sector 108 in our own full-frame photometry) and saw nothing, that choice is ruled out. 32 of 429 choices survive, the shortest being 25.9 days; the periods 25.9, 26.9, 33.6, 35.2, 37.1 days survive only because the transit they predict in sector 108 falls in a gap of our light curve. An earlier version of this report left out sector 108, which removes 11 more. Independent confirmation · Track B (long-period transits) · SPOC 2-minute data

HIP 71639: a warm Neptune every 23.9 days

Not a TOICommunity TOI since 2020Period posted May 2026SPOC TCE with a 31× wrong period

An F star at the edge of naked-eye visibility (V ≈ 6.1), 34 parsecs away in Lupus, dims by 642 parts per million for 7.2 hours every 23.8844 days. A planet about 4.1 times the size of Earth would do this.

Who saw it before

Volunteers of the citizen-science project Planet Hunters TESS spotted the sector-11 transit. Eisner et al. (2021, MNRAS 501, 4669) list the star as a single-transit planet candidate, and the project lists it among the community TOIs it sent to ExoFOP. The paper reports 43 MINERVA-Australis and 2 LCO/NRES spectra consistent with a planet and Gemini speckle imaging showing no nearby companion; the ground-based data were left for a later paper. CHIRON spectra followed in 2023.

On 15 May 2026 another ExoFOP user posted the period, P = 23.8843518 ± 0.0000098 d, from the same four transits in sectors 11, 38, 65 and 102. This survey found it independently in October and agrees within 0.1σ (23.8843529 ± 0.0000126 d). The planet is still not a TOI.

NASA's SPOC pipeline also detected the signal. Its multi-sector searches of sectors 1–39 and then 1–65 found the transits in sectors 11, 38 and 65 and reported a threshold-crossing event (multiple-event statistic 26.6). One transit fell in each sector, two years apart, so the pipeline took the gap between them as the period: 740.42 d. At that period the transit is far too short for the star (its fit implied a stellar density of 8.6 ± 6.5 ρ☉), and the centroid test was flagged. In the TOI lists available to us it never became a TOI.

The fourth transit, in sector 102 (April 2026), arrives 3 minutes from where 106 cycles of a 23.884-day period put it, and 740.42 days is exactly 31 of those cycles. What this page adds is the vetting below and the transit fit.

Vetting

  • ✓Passed:
    Four transits, one period. Transits in sectors 11, 38, 65 and 102 fit a single linear ephemeris. Every shorter alias (P/2 to P/8) predicts extra transits inside the observed data that are not there, ruled out at more than 39σ.
  • ✓Passed:
    The shape fits a planet on this star. Flat-bottomed, 7.22 ± 0.04 h long, with short ingress and egress. The stellar density the transit shape implies at this period, 0.36+0.05−0.09 ρ☉, is consistent with the catalogue value for this F star (0.30 ρ☉).
  • !Caution:
    Depths vary between sectors. In the summed pixels the four transits are 680, 454, 665 and 623 ppm deep: the sector-38 one is about 30% shallower, more than the errors allow. The star is saturated (T = 5.6), where light lost from the aperture along bleed columns varies from sector to sector; that is the likely cause but is not shown here. SPOC's odd-versus-even test passes.
  • ✓Passed:
    The signal is on this star. The dip is present in the summed flux of all pixels (680, 454, 665 and 623 ppm in the four sectors). The only Gaia source within 2′ bright enough to mimic it is TIC 128703032 (T = 10.6, 80″ away), which would need eclipses about 7% deep. Its brightest pixel changes by less than 0.25% at the transit times.
  • ✓Passed:
    No sign of a stellar companion. Gaia DR3: RUWE 0.94, no non-single-star solution, radial velocity stable to 0.13 km/s over 15 epochs.
  • !Caution:
    Centroids cannot help here. The 2023 DV report gives a 32″ offset of the difference-image centroid, but rates 0 of its 3 difference images as good quality: the star saturates the detector and bleeds along columns, which breaks centroiding. Our own centroids show the same, 15–58″ offsets that run along each sector's bleed column. The pixel test above does not rely on centroids.
  • iNote:
    Not yet ruled out: an eclipsing companion closer to the star than Gaia can separate from a G = 5.9 star. Eisner et al. (2021) report Gemini speckle imaging with no nearby companion and spectra consistent with a planet; those radial velocities, phased on the 23.88-day period, would also bound the planet's mass.

Period hunt · TOIs and community TOIs without a period

Periods for single-transit candidates, from every sector

ExoFOP lists 1003 TOIs and community TOIs (not false positives) with no period, most of them single transits. For each, every sector of TESS data was loaded (SPOC 2-minute, TESS-SPOC and QLP light curves, plus our own light curves of sectors 99–108 for 153 of them), the catalogued transit was re-found (810 of 1003 with data), other planets on the star were masked, and the rest of the data was searched for dips of the same duration and similar depth. Each new dip was then re-measured on its own sector's light curve against a local baseline and kept only if it is significant there, fully covered, away from sector edges and gaps, free of background jumps, present in the uncorrected (SAP) flux, and of the right depth. The periods that put all kept transits on one ephemeris without predicting transits the data do not show are listed.

390 targets gained at least one such transit, and 101 of those not on record are left with a single possible period. As a test: where someone has already posted a period for the same candidate to ExoFOP, it is among the hunt's allowed periods in 33 of 44 cases; of the other 11, 5 turn out to be errors in the posted value (see Corrections), 3 are periods the hunt's alias test wrongly excluded, and 3 are bookkeeping in confirmed multi-planet systems. No paper in NASA's ADS gives any of the new periods (full-text search by every SIMBAD identifier of each star, with the period itself), except for one eclipsing binary deeper than 1%, published in 2025, which is left out of the count. 126 periods are on no list and narrowed to three or fewer; 38 of them belong to dips deeper than 1%, mostly eclipsing binaries posted as community candidates, and are left out of the table (they are in period_review.csv). The table lists the rest, those on the star first, with the pixel test and, at each allowed period, the stellar density the transit shape implies against the catalogue value (a planet on that star at that period should match). TOI-2493.01, above, was checked one by one; the others are automated results. One caution: most of these stars have full-frame light curves that TESS's own pipelines search, and a strong periodic signal that never became a TOI may have been seen and rejected there, which is never published.

Periods and ephemerides that the TESS data contradict

Where someone had already posted a period for the same candidate, the period hunt disagreed in 11 cases. Measuring every transit that each period predicts settles them: in 3 the posted period is right and the hunt's alias test was too strict, 3 are bookkeeping in confirmed multi-planet systems, and in 5 the posted value is wrong for the transit it is attached to. A predicted transit counts as missing when the data cover it and show less than a third of the expected depth, more than 3σ below it; every corrected ephemeris was checked transit by transit in the light curves and at the pixel level.

CTOI 438225366.01: the period is 20.8615 days, not 41.72

Posted to ExoFOP in 2022 as a single transit with a period of 41.7219 days. That value joins the sector-6 and sector-33 transits as 18 cycles; the light curves show 8 transits of the same depth every 20.862 days, in sectors 6 to 87, 4 of them at the odd cycles where a 41.72-day period allows none. The odd and even transits have the same depth (7773 and 8026 ppm, 2.0σ apart), and half-way between them the light curve is flat (58 ± 70 ppm), so the 20.86-day period is not an eclipsing binary's primary and secondary at twice it.

The star is an A star (8,260 K, 2.2 R☉, T = 10.8, 650 pc), so a 0.83% transit means a companion of about 1.9 Jupiter radii: too large for a planet at this distance from the star unless the catalogue radius is too big, and likely a small star. The corrected period is what any follow-up needs either way.

Track B · deep vetting

The brightest remaining candidates, checked one by one

Every shortlisted star brighter than T = 10.5 went through the same checks as the headline candidates: Gaia binarity, TOI and SPOC lookups, the depth of each dip in every sector, the period aliases, and a pixel-level test of where the dip comes from (difference-image centroids, compared with every Gaia star within 150″ bright enough to fake the dip). 34 of 142 pass (69 off-target, 18 binary host, 16 ambiguous pixels, 5 no consistent period do not). Track B · single and duo transits

Other transit candidates

Every planet-sized dip on a dwarf star that passed the shape tests and is not a known TOI was followed up star by star: the dip finder was run on every TESS sector of the star, SPOC's own detections were read from the data-validation reports, and TOIs within 150″ were checked for a matching period. Of 2263 such stars, 636 turned out to be eclipsing binaries (deeper eclipses elsewhere in their data), 9 carry a TOI on a neighbouring star with the same period, and 7 became TOIs during 2026. Of the rest, 869 are left out here because they are larger than 16 Earth radii, repeat on periods shorter than 15 days, or were flagged in five or more SPOC searches without becoming TOIs (most likely rejected as binaries). 743 remain; the 60 with the strongest dips are listed below. Most are giant-sized and could be brown dwarfs or small stars; all need the pixel and period work done for the two headline candidates. The 60 listed were also searched on the web by TIC and Gaia number, and all 743 were matched by position against NASA's confirmed-planet table of 5 October 2026; each is labelled with what ExoFOP, SIMBAD, VizieR and NASA's archive already hold. 86 of the 743 are on record, most as single-transit community TOIs posted by the TESS Single Transit Planet Candidate working group or the Salinas et al. Track C · eclipsing triples and quadruples

Stars with two independent sets of eclipses

For every light curve with several deep dips, the main eclipsing period was found, a template of those eclipses was subtracted, and the residuals were searched for a second eclipse train. 4699 stars showed a strong second signal that is not a harmonic of the first and is eclipse-shaped (box depth at least three times the swing of a sine wave at that period, which rejects pulsations and spots). The table lists those whose second period recurs in two or more sectors.

A TESS pixel is 21″ wide, so two unrelated eclipsing binaries an arcminute apart blend into one light curve. Each eclipse set was therefore traced to its star: for every eclipse, the difference image (just before and after, minus during) shows only the light that disappeared, and a fitted point-spread function says where it sits, measured from the target's own image on that frame (Gaia positions moved to the date). Up to three sectors per star, every eclipse, combined with a 2″ systematic floor per sector. On 37 of Kostov et al.'s (2022) quadruple candidates (the other 31 lacked usable eclipses), whose authors traced both eclipse sets to one star with their own pixel analysis, the test puts both sets on the target for 36, a set on a neighbour for 0, and leaves 1 unresolved. Where ZTF's 1″ pixels reach (north of −30°), its light curves of the target and of every neighbour able to make the eclipses settle it directly: in 10 cases ZTF sees the eclipses on exactly the neighbour the centroids point to.

The outcome is clean and negative. All 6 systems that come out with both eclipse sets on the target are already published as quadruples, multiply eclipsing systems or a sextuple; every unpublished one (22) is a blend of two binaries on different stars, with the second star named in the table, and 2 could not be traced. Two of the blends are pairs of stars that move together on the sky, possibly wide binaries of binaries; their parallaxes disagree at 4–5σ, so this is not settled.

Track D · anomalies

Dippers and other odd dips

This track keeps dips that no planet makes: strongly asymmetric ones, and ones that last longer than 15 hours. 777 dips passed the anomaly cuts and the all-sector check. Looked at by eye, they fall into familiar groups: the known dipper HD 100546 (recovered), eclipses of a nearby binary leaking into a full-frame aperture, fast-rotating M dwarfs whose spots the detrending could not follow, very short-period binaries sampled into stroboscopic patterns, and long-period eclipsing binaries caught in one or two deep eclipses (TIC 304213412: 18-hour, 17%-deep eclipses repeating over six years; TIC 320329350: a 32%-deep, 17-hour eclipse). None is yet a convincing new kind of object, so none is claimed here.

Track A · small planets

A deeper search of nearby red dwarfs

4,936 K and M dwarfs within 150 parsecs, with years of TESS coverage but few two-minute sectors, are being searched with every sector stitched together, for planets down to about 1.5 Earth radii. 4,936 have been searched so far. Of 3,364 stars with a signal passing the search thresholds, 395 signals carry no red flag, against 126 in the same stars' flipped light curves (the flipped data contain no transits, so their flag-free signals measure the false-alarm rate). Above SDE 9.5, where 1 flipped signal is left, 27 real ones are on no TOI, community-TOI or NASA list. Two of them are TIC 4206066's planets, described in an independent manuscript and recovered here blind; one is a second, shorter signal on TIC 402710280 (3.784 days, about 349 ppm), the star of the new candidate above. The rest are being checked in the pixels and against today's ExoFOP before any is called a candidate. An earlier version of this triage read the depth at half the period as a secondary eclipse, which flagged nearly every real transit (TIC 4206066's planets among them); that is fixed.

In two-minute data the flipped light curves produced nothing, so planet-shaped dips there are essentially all astrophysical (transits or eclipses). In full-frame data the flipped rate varies by sector (median 1.0, highest 12.4 per 1000 in sector 70), driven by scattered light, which adds upward bumps that turn into dips when flipped. It is an upper bound: systematics are vetoed when they hit many stars at once, and flipping only 10% of light curves makes that veto ten times weaker for the flipped set. A single full-frame dip therefore needs the pixel and period checks before it counts. Method

How the search works

Data

Everything comes from the public MAST archive on Amazon S3: QLP full-frame light curves (sectors 1–98), SPOC two-minute light curves (to sector 107) with their data-validation reports, full-frame image cubes for pixel work, and the TESS Input Catalog and Gaia DR3. Light curves are read straight from the bucket; nothing is pre-selected by other pipelines.

Detrending and dips

Each light curve is binned to 10 minutes and flattened with a robust sliding biweight. A box filter at ten durations finds dips; each threshold uses the measured scatter of the filtered series itself, so slow correlated noise is counted. Upward outliers (flares) are clipped; downward points never are.

Systematics

Dips that strike many stars on the same CCD, or across the spacecraft, at the same time are vetoed. So are dips at data edges, in gaps, during background spikes, or absent from the raw aperture flux, and dips shared by nearby stars (only the deepest is kept).

Vetting

Survivors are re-fitted with trapezoid, asymmetric and Gaussian models; checked against every other sector of the star; matched against TOIs, SPOC detections and the literature; and examined in the pixels with difference images and Gaia neighbours.

Full details, thresholds and their sources: docs/METHODS.md. Code: pipeline/.

Turning a candidate into a planet

Publish it, then report it. Since 19 August 2026 ExoFOP accepts a new community candidate only after it is published in a peer-reviewed journal with online access, and only from users approved through its Published Candidate Upload Request; a Research Note of the AAS qualifies only if it cites a peer-reviewed paper describing the detection and vetting method, which this survey does not yet have. So TIC 402710280 cannot become a community TOI before a paper. What remains open to an ordinary account is adding parameter sets to existing objects: TOI-2493.01's period, HIP 56966's period and HD 11506's second transit on their community TOIs, and the corrections below, each as an alternative parameter set with a note (ExoFOP does not let one user edit another's entry). The values are in exofop_ready.md; ExoFOP staff should confirm that route first.

Test the predictions. TESS should catch TOI-2493.01 again on 15 November 2026 (sector 110). TIC 402710280's next transits (±20 minutes) fall in daylight or near the Sun from Chile until 3 May 2027, a full transit at night; TESS returns to it in sector 114, with a transit on 19 February 2027. A missing transit at the predicted time would kill the period.

Rule out impostors. HIP 71639 and HIP 56966 already have Gemini speckle imaging on ExoFOP showing no close companion. TIC 402710280, TOI-2493 and HD 11506 have none yet; they need speckle or adaptive-optics imaging and a few high-precision radial velocities (about 2–3 m/s for TIC 402710280).

Weigh it. HIP 71639's expected signal is about 3 m/s, well within reach of HARPS or ESPRESSO for a V = 6 star. A transit from CHEOPS, or the full 7-hour transit on 20 April or 14 May 2027 from Chile, would confirm the period.
